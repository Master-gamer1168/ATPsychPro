## How to Write Your Research Report
*AT Psychology · Group Project*

### Overview

Your report communicates your replication study the way psychologists share research with other experts: in a fixed, predictable format. Most of what goes into each section is already in your workbook. This guide shows you how to turn those notes into formal prose.

**Word count:** the main body (Introduction through Discussion) is 1,800–2,200 words. The title page, abstract, tables, figures, references and appendices do not count.

| Section | Suggested length | Workbook pages to use |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | 600–750 words | Original Study; Background Theory; Modifications; Aims, Variables and Hypotheses |
| Method | 400–500 words | Research Design; Sampling and Participants; Ethical Considerations; Procedures; Materials; Pilot Study |
| Results | 200–300 words (plus table and graph) | Descriptive Statistics; Graphing Data; Inferential Statistics |
| Discussion | 600–700 words | Comparison with original study and theory; Evaluation |

**Writing order vs. assembly order.** You write in one order and assemble in another.

| Order | |
|---|---|
| Writing order | Introduction → Method → (run the study) → Results → Discussion → Abstract last |
| Assembly order | Title page (p. 1) → Abstract (p. 2) → Introduction (p. 3+) → Method → Results → Discussion → References → Appendices |

**Report language.** Throughout the report:

- Write formally and concisely. Long-winded descriptions lose marks.
- Write in the third person. Do not use *I*, *we* or *they*; write *the researchers asked…* or *participants were required to…*
- Write in the past tense, as the study has already happened.
- Use correct research terminology (e.g., *independent measures design*, *operationalized*, *extraneous variable*). Your Research Methods, Experimental Design and Statistics guide is your reference.
- Write in paragraphs. Use bullet points only where this guide says you may.

## 1. Title Page (page 1)

The title page identifies your study. Your title should tell a reader exactly what was investigated, naming the operationalized IV and DV.

*Example: The Effect of Word Concreteness on Immediate Free Recall in High School Students*

- [ ] Page number 1 in the top-right header
- [ ] Descriptive title: centered, bold, in the upper half of the page
- [ ] Group members' names and block
- [ ] Course: AT Psychology, Singapore American School
- [ ] Submission month and year
- [ ] Word count of the main body

## 2. Abstract (page 2, written last)

The abstract is a single paragraph of 120–150 words that summarizes the whole study, so a reader can grasp it in under a minute. It does not count toward your word limit. Write it after everything else, and make sure its hypothesis and conclusion match the rest of your report.

**Format:** its own page after the title page · the bold, centered label **Abstract** · one double-spaced block paragraph · no first-line indent.

| Component (1–2 sentences each) | Sentence starter |
|---|---|
| Context and aim | This experiment replicated [anchor study] to investigate the effect of [IV] on [DV], based on [theory/model]… |
| Participants | A sample of [N] high school students was recruited using [sampling method]… |
| Method | …in an [independent / repeated measures] design. Participants completed [task] under either [condition A] or [condition B]… |
| Findings | Participants in [condition A] (M = [ ], SD = [ ]) [scored higher/lower] than those in [condition B] (M = [ ], SD = [ ]). A [test] indicated this difference was [significant / not significant] ([symbol] = [value], p [< / >] .05)… |
| Conclusion and application | These findings [support / do not support] the hypothesis and suggest that [theoretical implication or real-world application]. |

## 3. Introduction (about 600–750 words)

The Introduction explains the theory behind your study, the study you are replicating, and why your version looks the way it does. It ends with exactly what you tested and what you predicted. Focus on one theory or model and one anchor study (your key reading and key concept).

Write in paragraphs, in this order. Each part should lead logically into the next. Cite every theory and study in APA format.

### Part 1: The theory or model (about 150–200 words)

Introduce the phenomenon your study investigates and the theory or model that explains it.

- Name the phenomenon in plain terms (e.g., *people remember the first and last items of a list better than the middle*).
- Name the theory or model, with a citation, and summarize its key points.
- Define any key terms a reader needs to follow the rest of the report.

*Workbook: Background Theory or Model.*

### Part 2: The anchor study (about 200–250 words)

Summarize the study you are replicating. Cover four things:

1. **Aim:** what the researchers set out to investigate.
2. **Method:** design, participants, IV, DV and the core task.
3. **Findings:** what they found, with key numbers where available.
4. **Link to the theory:** what the findings show about the theory or model from Part 1.

The fourth point is the one students most often leave out. Spell out *why* the result supports (or challenges) the theory.

*Workbook: Original Study Being Replicated.*

### Part 3: Justifying the replication and modifications (about 150–200 words)

Explain why your study is worth running and how it differs from the original.

- **Why replicate:** state why it is worth testing this finding again with your sample (e.g., checking whether the effect holds with high school students in Singapore, or in a simplified classroom setting).
- **What you changed:** describe each major modification to the original study.
- **Why you changed it:** name the modification category and justify it. Show that the change keeps the core phenomenon intact.

| Modification category | Typical reason |
|---|---|
| Ethical protection | Protects participants from distress, embarrassment or deception |
| Methodological control | Removes confounding variables, order effects or carryover effects |
| Construct adaptation and access | Replaces an inaccessible population or apparatus with a feasible analogue |
| Construct validity and measurement | Turns qualitative observations into a measurable DV |
| Feasibility and standardization | Fits the school timetable and gives every participant identical conditions |

*Workbook: Modifying the Original Study. See also the Designing Experimental Replications guide.*

### Part 4: Aim, variables and hypotheses (subheadings allowed)

End the Introduction with your aim, variables and hypotheses. You may set these out under short subheadings.

- **Aim:** one sentence stating what your study investigates. *To investigate the effect of [IV] on [DV] in [population].*
- **Independent variable:** name it and give its operational definition, including both conditions.
- **Dependent variable:** name it and give its operational definition, stating exactly how it was measured.
- **Research hypothesis (H₁):** a directional prediction naming the population, the operationalized IV and DV, and the expected outcome. *High school students who [condition A] will [score higher on DV] than those who [condition B].*
- **Null hypothesis (H₀):** *there will be no significant difference in [DV] between [condition A] and [condition B]; any difference is due to chance.*

Your hypothesis must follow from Parts 1–3. A reader should be able to see why the theory and anchor study lead to this prediction.

*Workbook: Aims and Variables; Hypotheses.*

## 4. Method (about 400–500 words)

The Method describes exactly how the study was conducted, in enough detail that another group could repeat it. Use the four subheadings below, in this order, and write each in paragraphs.

### Design

- State the method (e.g., laboratory experiment) and the design (independent measures, repeated measures or matched pairs).
- Explain why you chose this design, naming the specific extraneous variable it controls (e.g., order effects, participant variables, expectancy effects).
- State how you controlled the design's weakness (random allocation, counterbalancing or matching).

*Workbook: Research Design.*

### Participants

- Describe the sample: size (N), age, gender, and any other relevant characteristic (e.g., language). Use your actual sample, not the proposed one.
- State the target population and the sampling method, how participants were recruited, and why you chose that method. Practical reasons are acceptable.
- Justify at least one sample characteristic, ideally as a control for an extraneous variable (e.g., *all participants were fluent English readers, so reading ability did not confound recall*).
- Explain how participants were treated ethically: informed consent, right to withdraw, confidentiality and debriefing. Name any deception and how it was handled.

*Workbook: Sampling and Participants; Ethical Considerations.*

### Materials

- List the materials used, each with a reference to its appendix (e.g., *the word list (see Appendix A)*). Leave out everyday items such as pens and chairs.
- Explain at least one material your group created, and how it controlled an extraneous variable (e.g., *words were matched for length and frequency across both lists*).

*Workbook: Materials.*

### Procedure

- Describe each step in the order it happened, from greeting participants to debriefing them.
- Write in the past tense, third person and in paragraphs: *Participants were randomly allocated to…; the researcher read the standardized instructions (see Appendix B)…*
- Include timings, the instructions given, and how the DV was recorded.
- If your pilot study led to changes, mention them briefly here.

*Workbook: Procedures; Pilot Study.*

## 5. Results (about 200–300 words, plus table and graph)

The Results report what you found, without interpreting it. Save all explanation for the Discussion.

1. **How the DV was measured:** one to three sentences (e.g., *recall was scored as the number of words correctly written in two minutes*).
2. **Descriptive statistics:** a summary table of the measure of central tendency and dispersion for each condition (e.g., mean and standard deviation), followed by a sentence stating which condition scored higher.
3. **Graph:** one appropriate graph (usually a bar chart of the two condition means), with a descriptive title, both axes labelled, and axes that are not distorted. Add a one-sentence summary beneath it.
4. **Inferential statistics:** state the test and why you chose it (one sentence), report the calculated value, critical value and N, and state whether the null hypothesis was accepted or rejected. *A Mann-Whitney U test was used because the design was independent measures and the data were ordinal (U = 12, critical value = 23, N = 20, p < .05). The null hypothesis was therefore rejected.*

Do not include raw data here. Raw scores and step-by-step calculations go in Appendix E.

*Workbook: Descriptive Statistics; Graphing Data; Inferential Statistics.*

## 6. Discussion (about 600–700 words)

The Discussion interprets your results and judges what they mean. Write it in paragraphs, following the structure below.

### Paragraph 1: Your findings and the hypothesis

- Restate your hypothesis and say whether it was supported or not supported.
- Restate the key results with numbers (means and the inferential test outcome).
- Explain *why* the hypothesis was or was not supported, linking directly to the results.
- Identify any unexpected results or individual outliers, and suggest how they may have affected the outcome.

### Paragraph 2: Comparison with the anchor study and the theory

- Compare your results with the anchor study from your Introduction. Are they in the same direction? Is the effect a similar size?
- If your results differ, explain what might account for it: your modifications, sample or procedure.
- Explain what your results suggest about the theory or model (support, challenge or partly support it). Cite the sources from your Introduction.

### Paragraph 3: Limitations and improvements

- Identify two limitations of your study, considering reliability and validity. Look at the sample (is it representative?), the design and the procedure.
- For each limitation, explain how it may have affected your results and suggest a specific modification that would overcome it.

### Paragraph 4: Conclusion and implications

- State a specific conclusion for your sample.
- State whether your conclusion can be generalized beyond your sample, given your limitations.
- Justify at least one real-world application or implication of your findings.
- Suggest one direction for further research.

*Workbook: Written Summary of Results (comparison with the original study and theory); Evaluation.*

## 7. References and In-Text Citations

Cite every theory, model and study you mention, using APA 7th edition. At minimum, this means your anchor study and the source for your theory or model.

### In-text citations

- Parenthetical: (Author, Year) or (Author & Author, Year).
- Three or more authors: (Author et al., Year).
- Narrative: *According to Loftus and Palmer (1974)…*

### Reference list

- [ ] Starts on a new page after the Discussion, under the bold, centered heading **References**
- [ ] Alphabetized by the first author's last name
- [ ] Hanging indent (first line flush left, later lines indented 0.5 inches)
- [ ] Double-spaced, with no extra blank lines between entries
- [ ] Every reference matches an in-text citation, and every citation has a reference

## 8. Appendices

Appendices hold the materials and evidence a reader needs to check or repeat your study. Start each on a new page after the References, with a bold, centered label (e.g., **Appendix A: Materials**). Refer to every appendix in the body of your report; an appendix that is never mentioned should not be there.

| Appendix | Contents |
|---|---|
| A: Materials | Word lists, images, questionnaires, test sheets or slides for both conditions |
| B: Standardized instructions | The verbatim script given to all participants |
| C: Informed consent form | Voluntary participation, confidentiality and right to withdraw |
| D: Debriefing statement | The script explaining the true aim of the study |
| E: Raw data and calculations | Every participant's score, followed by step-by-step inferential test calculations |

## 9. Final Submission Checklist

- [ ] Pages are in assembly order: Title page → Abstract → Introduction → Method → Results → Discussion → References → Appendices
- [ ] Font: Times New Roman 12 pt, Arial 11 pt or Calibri 11 pt
- [ ] Double-spaced throughout, with 1-inch (2.54 cm) margins
- [ ] Page numbers in the top-right header, starting on page 1
- [ ] Main body (Introduction to Discussion) is 1,800–2,200 words
- [ ] Introduction covers the theory, the anchor study, the justification for modifications, and the aim, variables and hypotheses
- [ ] Hypothesis is worded the same way in the Introduction, Discussion and Abstract
- [ ] Discussion justifies at least one real-world implication
- [ ] Written in the formal third person and past tense
