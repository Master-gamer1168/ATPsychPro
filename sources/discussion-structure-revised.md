# Discussion Structure (REVISED): authoritative

> **Source:** provided directly on 23 Nov 2026, to be kept as the Discussion structure for the report.
> **Status:** this **supersedes §6 "Discussion" of the [guide](guide-how-to-write-research-report.md)**. Paragraphs 1 and 2 match the guide word for word. Paragraphs 3 and 4 are stricter (see the comparison below).
> The text between the rules is verbatim.

---

### Paragraph 1: Your findings and the hypothesis

* Restate your hypothesis and say whether it was supported or not supported.
* Restate the key results with numbers (means and the inferential test outcome).
* Explain why the hypothesis was or was not supported, linking directly to the results.
* Identify any unexpected results or individual outliers, and suggest how they may have affected the outcome.

### Paragraph 2: Comparison with the anchor study and the theory

* Compare your results with the anchor study from your Introduction. Are they in the same direction? Is the effect a similar size?
* If your results differ, explain what might account for it: your modifications, sample or procedure.
* Explain what your results suggest about the theory or model (support, challenge or partly support it). Cite the sources from your Introduction.

### Paragraph 3: Limitations and improvements

* Identify two specific limitations of your study's design, materials, or procedure. (Note: Do not rely solely on "small sample size" or "lack of ecological validity." If you mention either, push past the obvious label to explain the specific cognitive or behavioral mechanism affected, and ensure at least one limitation focuses on internal validity or reliability—such as ceiling/floor effects, order effects, demand characteristics, or inconsistent testing conditions.)
* For each limitation, explain how it distorted your specific results (linking to your means, spread, or outliers from Paragraph 1) and propose a concrete procedural or material modification to fix it.

### Paragraph 4: Conclusion and implications

* State a specific conclusion supported by your data.
* Evaluate how far your conclusion can be generalized—going beyond simply calling the sample small or the task artificial by identifying one real-world situation your task mirrors well and one situation where additional real-world factors would change the outcome.
* Justify at least one real-world application or implication of your findings.
* Suggest one direction for further research.
