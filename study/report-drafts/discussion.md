**Discussion**

The research hypothesis, that high school students will give a higher overall rating of likeability and quality to a song they are told took 1 week to make than to a song they are told took 2 days to make, was supported. Songs described as taking 1 week received higher combined ratings (*M* = 7.06, *SD* = 1.10) than songs described as taking 2 days (*M* = 6.08, *SD* = 1.31), and a Wilcoxon signed-ranks test showed that this difference was significant (*W* = 18, critical value = 110, *N* = 29, *p* < .01). As only the stated production time differed between conditions, the higher ratings can be attributed to perceived effort. Ratings in the 2-day condition were also more widely spread, partly because one participant gave the 2-day song a combined rating of only 3. Unexpectedly, the only two participants who rated the 2-day song higher belonged to the same group of eight, whose mean difference (0.56 points) was about half that of the other groups (1.00 to 1.25), slightly reducing the overall effect.

These findings were in the same direction as those of Kruger et al. (2004), who found that participants told a poem took 18 hours to write rated it significantly higher in liking and quality than those told it took 4 hours. **[INSERT: Kruger et al.'s condition means, and whether their effect was larger or smaller than here.]** In this study, stated effort raised ratings by about one point on an 11-point scale. The effect held despite three modifications: songs replaced a poem, high school students replaced university students, and a repeated measures design replaced independent groups. A supplementary comparison of participants who heard the same song under different labels was also significant for both songs (*U* = 77 and *U* = 69.5, critical value = 83, *p* < .05), suggesting that the effect did not depend on hearing both labels. The results therefore support the effort heuristic (Kruger et al., 2004): when the quality of an unfamiliar song was difficult to judge, participants appear to have substituted the easier question of how much effort it required. Ignoring the labels, the two songs were rated almost identically (6.53 and 6.61).

Two limitations should be considered. First, the repeated measures design may have introduced demand characteristics, reducing internal validity. Despite filler details, every participant read both production times, and this contrast may have revealed the aim. Participants who guessed it could have raised their rating of the 1-week song to match the expected result, which is consistent with no individual rating shifting by more than 2 points, and may have inflated the consistency of the 0.98-point mean difference. An independent measures design, in which each participant hears one song with one production time, would remove the contrast, and a final question about the perceived aim would allow guessers to be excluded. Second, testing conditions may have varied between group sessions, reducing reliability. The group with a mean difference of only 0.56 points contained both reversals, suggesting that something about that session, such as the delivery of instructions, the song order or reactions from peers, weakened the manipulation. Pre-recorded instructions, headphones at separated desks, and recording the session and song order on every answer sheet would standardize testing.

For this sample of 32 high school students, describing a song as taking 1 week rather than 2 days to make led to significantly higher ratings of its likeability and quality. The task closely mirrors hearing an unfamiliar song once on a streaming platform or social media post whose caption states how long it took to produce, where listeners have few other quality cues. The outcome would likely change for listeners with musical training or for songs by well-known artists, because expertise, reputation and reviews would provide stronger cues to quality. Since 27 of 32 participants rated an identical song higher when told it took more effort, judges of creative work, such as competition panels or art teachers, should assess work without knowing how long it took. Further research could test whether musical expertise reduces the effect by comparing music students with non-music students.
