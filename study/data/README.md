## Design (from the workbook)

- **N = 32**: 4 data sheets × 8 participants (high school students in advisory groups).
- **IV:** stated production time of a song, **1 week** (high effort) vs **2 days** (low effort).
- **DV:** mean of **likeability** (1 = hate, 11 = love) and **quality** (1 = bad, 11 = great). This is the "combined" score. The two ratings are also analysed separately.
- **Design:** **repeated measures**. Each participant heard both songs (**SoR** and **CYFI**), one under each label.
- **Label counterbalancing:** sheets 1 and 4 labelled SoR as 1 week; sheets 2 and 3 labelled CYFI as 1 week.
- **Difference** = 1 week − 2 days. Positive means the high-effort song was rated higher.
- **Not in the data:** play order, advisory, version X/Y, who guessed the aim, demographics. See issues 4, 5 and 9 in [`../README.md`](../README.md), section "Inconsistencies and gaps".

| Sign test (exact, one-tailed) | *p* < .00001 | *p* = .00027 | *p* < .00001 |
| Effect size (rank-biserial *r*) | .92 (large) | .83 (large) | .85 (large) |

*N* for the Wilcoxon = participants with a non-zero difference; ties are excluded.

**Between-student check** (workbook p. 6): holding the song constant and comparing students told "1 week" with students told "2 days" (Mann-Whitney U, n = 16 per group):
- **Combined DV: significant for both songs.** SoR *U* = 77; CYFI *U* = 69.5. Critical value 83, *p* < .05.
- Liking for SoR alone just misses: *U* = 84 against a critical value of 83.

**Bottom line:** participants rated the song described as taking longer to make significantly higher, on the combined measure and on each rating separately. The effect holds whichever song carried the "1 week" label, and the two songs were rated almost identically overall (combined *M* = 6.53 vs 6.61), so song choice doesn't explain it.

## Which test?

- **Wilcoxon signed-rank on the combined DV is the main test.** It matches the design (repeated measures), the data (ordinal ratings) and H₁ (directional, on overall likeability and quality).
- **Chi-square** is in RESULTS.md because you asked for it. It counts how many participants preferred the high-effort song versus the low-effort one and compares that with a 50/50 split. Chi-square is meant for **nominal frequency data from independent observations**, and here it ignores the size of each difference. Use it only if your teacher or workbook specified it; if so, justify it as preferences categorised as nominal (higher/lower).
- **Mann-Whitney (between-student)** is a supporting analysis that the workbook planned. It is not the main test.

### Reporting sentences (adapt; these follow the guide's Results format)

> Each participant's score was the mean of their likeability and quality ratings (1–11) for each song. A one-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used because the hypothesis was directional, the design was repeated measures and the ratings were treated as ordinal (*T* = 18, critical value = 110, *N* = 29, *p* < .01; three participants with tied scores were excluded). The null hypothesis was therefore rejected.

## Hooks for the Discussion (revised structure)

These are observations from the data, not written paragraphs. Check them against [`../../sources/discussion-structure-revised.md`](../../sources/discussion-structure-revised.md).

- **¶1 outliers / unexpected:**
  - **Sheet 1 is weaker than the others.** Combined mean difference is 0.56, against 1.00–1.25 elsewhere. It contains **both combined-score reversals** (S1-P3, S1-P8) and all 3 quality reversals. It is the only sheet that isn't significant on its own.
  - **S1-P7** gave the single lowest rating (quality = 2).
  - **Low-effort ratings are more spread out** than high-effort ones (combined *SD* 1.31 vs 1.10).
- **¶2 vs anchor:**
  - Same direction as Kruger et al. (2004): higher effort produced higher liking and quality.
  - The effect is about **1 point on an 11-point scale**. To compare size, you need the original study's means (see issue 6 in [`../README.md`](../README.md)).
  - Two features of this replication strengthen the case for the effort heuristic: the song was held constant in the between-student check, and the two songs were rated almost identically overall.
- **¶3 limitation ideas (internal validity / reliability, tied to the numbers):**
  - *Demand characteristics from repeated measures:* each participant saw both production times, so the 1-week vs 2-day contrast may have revealed the aim, despite the filler details. That could produce a uniform "polite" bump. Every liking and quality difference is between −1 and +2, and 27 of 32 participants moved in the same direction.
    *Concrete fix:* a between-subjects version, with each student hearing one song and one label. The between-student check suggests the effect survives even then.
  - *Inconsistent testing conditions across sessions:* sheet 1's weaker effect may reflect something about that session (advisory, time of day, instructions, play order). Group testing also lets participants influence each other, as the workbook's own limitation note says.
    *Concrete fix:* a single recorded instruction script, a fixed playback setup, and logging the session and play order on every sheet.
  - *Halo between the DVs:* liking and quality correlate strongly (ρ = .77), so participants may not have judged them separately.
    *Concrete fix:* define each rating with anchor descriptions on the answer sheet, and separate the two questions.
  - **No ceiling effect.** No 10s or 11s were given, so don't use a ceiling effect as a limitation.
- **¶4 generalisation:**
  - *Mirrors well:* first exposure to an unfamiliar song or artwork where effort information is stated, e.g. a streaming or social-media post saying "took a week to produce", or a craft fair label.
  - *Would change:* listeners with musical expertise or strong genre preferences, songs by known artists (reputation and price cues), or effort claims that aren't credible. In these cases the heuristic is less needed or is overridden.
